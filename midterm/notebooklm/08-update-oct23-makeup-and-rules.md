## The 2025 makeup paper in brief

Exhibit: two charts. Left: share of working age adults with a university degree across OECD countries; Canada tops the chart at nearly two thirds, Germany has 34.3%. Right: productivity, 2000 to 2023; in 2023 Germany's productivity is over 30% higher than Canada's, and the gap grew over the period.

1. Define the puzzle (10 marks, about 100 words). Education is human capital, a driver of productivity in the course growth model, yet the most educated country has lower and slower-growing productivity than a much less degree-heavy one. Name both countries and the numbers. Do not explain the cause.
2. Three distinct hypotheses for Canada (15 marks, 3 x 5, about 150 words). Key options: weak capital deepening and business investment (educated workers without tools); industrial structure tilted to resources and low-productivity services; weak diffusion and adoption of technology and management practices; weak competition and small markets slowing creative destruction; skill mismatch and overqualification; poor coordination between education and industry. Pick three that are clearly different and tie each to a chart and a growth model term.
3. Germany, "makes the thing that makes the thing" (10 marks, about 100 words). A deep base of firms making machines, tools and components; a workforce with strong technical and vocational training rather than degrees; skills matched to production, so training turns into output per hour. Link both charts and talk only about Germany.
4. Prime Minister Carney asks: cut basic university research and focus on adopting ideas from abroad? Start with "Yes, good idea" or "No, bad idea" (15 marks, about 150 words). The key gives model answers for both sides. Yes: fiscal pressure, Canada's problem is diffusion and execution rather than discovery, knowledge spills across borders, redirect to applied work, make it temporary. No: research is an investment that raises future productivity and revenue, basic science builds absorptive capacity (you need researchers to adopt foreign ideas well), brain drain risk, fix the lab-to-market link instead, cut operating spending instead.

## The pattern across all three past papers

Each paper gives one exhibit and four to six short written answers with word caps. The questions climb the same ladder:
1. Describe or define what the exhibit shows, with its numbers (2025: the crop yield puzzle; 2024: the trilemma; makeup: the education versus productivity puzzle).
2. Explain it with the course growth model: labour force growth plus productivity growth (capital deepening, labour quality, innovation), with institutions underneath.
3. Apply it to a country, usually Canada, using class facts.
4. Give advice on a policy, starting with a clear position, and defend it with a named course concept. Either side can earn full marks if the reasoning is right.

## Trade and China (Sessions 9 and 10), in case they appear

Trade sits in the growth model's institutional environment. Economists see most gains from trade on the import side, not the export side. Eight ways openness can raise growth: competitive spur and creative destruction, specialization and scale, infusion of ideas and technology, cheaper and better imported capital goods, learning by exporting, global value chains, pressure to build human capital, and external pressure that improves institutions. Industrial policy uses subsidies, incentives, regulation and R&D funding to steer resources to strategic sectors.

China: strong on the three i's (investment, infusion, innovation), yet growth has slowed from near 10% a year before 2010, with the trend falling about a third of a point a year. Strengths: closing in on the AI frontier, leading electric and green industries. Weaknesses: collapsed property, prices falling as output outruns demand, nervous households, a huge trade surplus. History suggests regression to the mean: fast growers slow toward about 2% a year per person.
