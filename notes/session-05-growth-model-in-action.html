Welcome to session five. The primers told us GDP grows from the supply side. Today we ask why some countries build that supply side and others never do.

The whole session, in one picture

In one line
GDP grows from the supply side: more capital, more skills, better productivity. Institutions decide whether anyone bothers to build those, because they decide who keeps the gains.
The move that earns marks. For every Canada or US item you raise, say which link it breaks: power, rules, or incentives. That turns an opinion into an argument about growth.

2 Inclusive vs extractive

In one line
Growth happens when anyone can build and keep what they build, which only lasts when political power is spread out and the state is strong enough to enforce the rules.
InclusiveExtractive
PoliticalPluralist and centralisedPower in a narrow elite
EconomicSecure property, open entry, fair courtsSeizure, monopolies, rigged licences
What paysProducing and innovatingGetting close to power
ExampleSouth Korea since 1960sNorth Korea
Why the pair matters. Economic rules are chosen by whoever holds political power. So inclusive economics cannot survive long without inclusive politics. That is the link the US question tests.

3 Why bad rules persist

In one line
Elites block growth on purpose, because creative destruction would destroy their power as well as old firms. Poverty is a political choice, not a knowledge gap.
IdeaExample from the book
Critical junctureBlack Death: Western serfdom weakened, Eastern tightened
Elites fear changeAustria-Hungary and Russia slowed railways
Extractive growth fadesSoviet boom, then stagnation by 1970s
Iron law of oligarchyPost-colonial leaders reused colonial extraction
The China point. Their prediction: extractive growth can catch up, but it stalls at the frontier without open entry and constrained power. Worth raising, since China is the usual objection.

4 The narrow corridor (optional podcast)

In one line
Liberty and growth live in a narrow corridor where the state and society are both strong and keep pushing against each other. Leave it either way and things slide.
Use this for the US. The worry is a strong executive pulling away from the courts, press and agencies meant to restrain it. That is a move toward the despotic side.

6 The US stress test

In one line
The concern is political institutions being weakened one check at a time, which Acemoglu and Robinson predict eventually bends the economic rules too.
Moves reported in 2025, sorted by which check they weaken
CheckMoveGrowth channel

What they would say

The danger is capture of the political side. Once courts, data and money answer to one person, economic rules become favours. Expect weaker investment and innovation over years, not quarters.

Why it may be overstated

Courts still rule against the administration, states hold power, the press is free, and the 2026 midterms can change Congress. The corridor idea says society can push back.

My position. The argument is right about direction and probably overstated on speed. The US has deep buffers, but “richest third world country” is a warning about trend, not a description of today.

Items are 2025 events to check against current news before citing them in class. Court outcomes on tariffs and firings may have changed since.

7 What to say when called on

Sixty seconds, out loud

Proposition 1 says growth comes from the supply side, but Acemoglu and Robinson ask why some countries build capital and ideas and others do not. Their answer is rules: inclusive institutions let anyone invest and keep the return, and they only last when political power is spread out. On that scorecard Canada passes the big tests, courts, elections, a central bank nobody interferes with. Where it needs work is competition and building: concentrated telecoms and banks, provincial trade barriers, and slow permits. Those protect incumbents, and that shows up in our weak productivity. The US is the harder case. Pressure on the Fed, firing the statistics chief, tariffs by emergency decree and deals for favoured firms all weaken the political checks. Their theory says the economic rules follow. I think the direction is right but the speed is overstated, because US courts, states and elections still push back.

The prep questions, answered

Question, as set
How would you rate Canada from this institutional perspective? What would be on your scorecard in terms of "needs improvement" for Canada?
Canada

Strongly inclusive, with a competition problem. Top marks on courts, elections, property and the central bank. Needs improvement: concentrated sectors, interprovincial barriers, slow approvals, and unsettled Indigenous title. Each shields incumbents from new entrants, so it drags on productivity. §5.

Question, as set
What do you see as Trump's most dangerous institution-degrading "moves"? What would Robinson and Acemoglu say about the US's future economic prospects if Trump gets his way? Do you agree, or do you think maybe the argument is overstated?
United States

Most dangerous: attacks on the referees. Fed independence and honest statistics, because every investor relies on them, and bypassing Congress on tariffs. Their prediction: political capture, then economic rules that reward connections, then slower innovation. Partly agree: the direction is right, but courts, federalism and elections make it slower and reversible. §6.

Q&A log

Asked in prep
If US institutions are eroding, why are US stocks and growth still strong?

Institutions work on growth over decades. The stock of trust, capital and talent built over 200 years does not vanish in a year. The theory predicts slower investment and innovation later, which markets may not price until it shows up.

The line to say

Institutions are a stock, not a flow. You can spend them down for a while before the bill arrives.
