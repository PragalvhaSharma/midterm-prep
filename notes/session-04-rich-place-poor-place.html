The whole session, in one picture

In one line
Rich countries are not rich because they have more stuff. They are rich because their stuff and their people produce more, and what decides that is the rules they live under.
The move that earns marks. When someone names a cause, ask which column it sits in. Most answers in class will be middle-column answers ("they need more capital"), which just restates the question. The interesting answer is always why the middle column is empty.

1 How big the gap really is

In one line
Take output per person, reprice it so a haircut costs the same everywhere, and the top of the table is roughly 40 to 50 times the bottom.

The same bottom five, rescaled

Do the ratios yourself
Canada ÷ Malawi = 67,013 ÷ 1,758 = 38.1×
United States ÷ Malawi = 89,991 ÷ 1,758 = 51.2×
United States ÷ Burundi = 89,991 ÷ 994 = 90.5×

The class figure of 40 to 50 fold is the middle of that range. Take the extremes and you get past 100.

Say it in human terms. 38 times is not "Canadians have nicer phones". It is the difference between a life where a bad harvest is an inconvenience and one where it is a funeral. Malawi's poverty rate is projected at 76.6% for 2026, and 76% live on under $3 a day.

2 The three inputs, and why two of them cannot explain it

In one line
Output comes from machines, skills, and how well they are combined. The first two are nowhere near enough to explain a 38× gap, so the third is doing the work.

Why capital alone is a dead end

Capital has diminishing returns. With the standard one-third exponent, output rises with the cube root of capital, so the arithmetic gets absurd fast.

1

Double the machines, see what you get

Hold skills and productivity fixed, give every worker twice the equipment.

Y ∝ K⅓
Output multiple = 2⅓
                = 1.26, i.e. 26% more output   for 100% more capital
2

Now run it backwards to close the Canada gap

If capital were the only difference, how much more of it would Malawi need? Invert the exponent.

Y multiple needed = 38.1
K multiple        = 38.13
                  = 38.1 × 38.1 × 38.1
                  = 55,306× the capital per worker

Fifty-five thousand times. No country has ever been short of capital by that factor, and no amount of aid or foreign investment could supply it. Capital is not the explanation.

3

Schooling has the same problem, in miniature

Malawi averages 4.7 years of schooling, Canada roughly 13.5. An extra year of school is usually worth about 8% more earnings, so run the whole gap through that.

Extra years        = 13.5 − 4.7 = 8.8
Output multiple    = 1.088.8
                   = about 2.0×   illustrative, standard Mincer return

Doubling. Against a gap of 38 times. Add capital's plausible contribution and you have explained maybe a quarter of it.

4

So the residual is the story

Whatever is left after K and H is A: productivity. The question "why are some places poor" is really "why is A so low", and A is about rules, not resources.

The one-third exponent and the 8% return to a year of schooling are the standard textbook values, used here to show the shape of the argument. Treat the 2.0× as illustrative, not as a Malawi measurement.

4 One town, one fence, two incomes

In one line
Nogales, Arizona and Nogales, Sonora are the same town split by a border. Same desert, same people, same food, and incomes several times apart.

Inclusive institutions

Broad property rights, open entry, courts that bind the powerful too.

  • You invest because you keep the gains
  • New firms enter and old ones can fail
  • Talent sorts by ability, not connection

Extractive institutions

Rules that move income to whoever holds power.

  • You do not invest because it can be taken
  • Entry is blocked to protect incumbents
  • Talent sorts into getting permissions
The mechanism, concretely

A Malawian trader who could earn a fortune importing fertiliser at the official rate is not producing fertiliser. She is producing an application. The talent is real, the effort is real, the output is zero. That is what low A looks like on the ground.

5 Catching up is arithmetically possible and historically rare

In one line
A poor country can grow faster than a rich one because copying beats inventing. Most do not, and the ones that do not fall further behind every year.

The one fact that makes catch-up believable

Korea and Ghana

In 1960 South Korea and Ghana had roughly the same income per person. Korea had no oil, no diamonds, no coastline advantage over Ghana, and had just been flattened by a war.

2025: Korea $65,405  vs  Ghana $8,613 = 7.6×

Nothing about the land changed. Everything about the rules did. This is the single best rebuttal to "they are poor because of where they are".

8 What is constraining growth

In one line
Six things are wrong. One of them, the price of foreign exchange, is stopping the other five from being fixable.
1
Foreign exchange, the binding oneExports of $880m cannot pay for an economy's imports. The official rate has been frozen at 1,716 since March 2024 while prices rose 28%, so the kwacha is badly overvalued. Dollars are rationed by permission. Firms that could produce cannot buy inputs.
2
One crop carries the countryTobacco is 55% of goods exports, in a world actively trying to smoke less. A bad season is not a bad season, it is a balance of payments crisis.
3
The state is borrowing what firms needA deficit of 8.4% of GDP, financed at home, pushes up yields and crowds private borrowers out. Debt distress since 2022 means no cheap money from abroad either.
4
Farming is rain-fed and flatOver 80% of workers, almost no irrigation, depleted soils, and a fertiliser subsidy that eats the budget without lifting yields much. Cyclone Freddy in 2023 showed how thin the margin is.
5
Power and distance15.6% of people on the grid, hydro that fails in drought, and 800 km of bad road to the nearest port. Every imported input arrives dear and every export leaves dear.
6
The denominator keeps growingPopulation up 2.6% a year with 41% under 14. The economy has to grow 2.6% just to stand still, and it grew 1.9%.

Why number one is the binding one

1

Look for the highest shadow price

The binding constraint is whatever people are paying the biggest premium to get around. In Malawi that is a dollar.

Official rate   = 1,716 MWK per USD      frozen since Mar 2024
Street rate     = far above it, depending on the week
Premium         = the pure profit in holding an import licence

Nobody pays a premium for schooling or roads. They pay it for dollars.

2

Check the chain it breaks

Trace one input through. The farmer is not short of ambition, and the constraint bites three steps upstream of him.

No dollars  →  no fertiliser imported
            →  yields fall
            →  less to eat and less to sell
            →  less foreign currency next year
            →  fewer dollars still

It is a loop, not a line. That is why it has not self-corrected.

3

Confirm the other constraints are downstream

Build a road and the trucks still need diesel you cannot import. Train a machinist and the factory still cannot buy the machine. Every other fix runs through the same missing dollar. That is the test of a binding constraint, and this one passes it.

Careful with the word cause. The dollar shortage is the binding constraint, not the deep cause. The deep cause is a rule that sets a price by decree and hands out the difference. Say both.

In one line

What Malawi could plausibly sell

Minerals

Large rutile and graphite deposits at Kasiya. One mine could exceed total current goods exports.

  • Needs power, rail and a credible contract regime

Higher value crops

Soy, pigeon pea, groundnut, macadamia, tea. Less exposed than tobacco and already grown.

  • Needs irrigation, storage and predictable export rules

Labour-intensive assembly

The Bangladesh route. Wages are low enough; almost nothing else is in place.

  • Needs power, ports and an importable input at a real price
Do not promise the mine as the answer. A landlocked country with weak contracts and no power is exactly where a resource find turns into a rent to be fought over. The mine only helps after steps 1 to 3, which is the same institutions argument again.

10 Why it hasn't happened

In one line
Everybody in Lilongwe knows the diagnosis. The reform concentrates its losses on people with power and spreads its gains across people without any.
The rents are the point
A frozen rate is not an accident. Whoever is allocated dollars at 1,716 pockets the gap. Floating the currency deletes that income overnight, and the people holding it are close to the people deciding.
Fuel and maize are the two most visible prices
A devaluation shows up at the pump and in the fertiliser bag within days. The benefit, an export sector, shows up after the next election. No government survives that trade voluntarily.
The subsidy is the state's one visible service
For 80% of the population the fertiliser programme is what government is. Cutting it to fund irrigation is correct and unelectable.
Aid removes the deadline
Inflows have averaged around 7% of GDP a year for decades. Enough to avoid collapse, never enough to force the reform. The crisis is chronic, so it is never urgent.
Reform needs the thing reform destroys
A float without reserves overshoots and inflation spikes. Reserves need a programme, a programme needs reform. The lapsed 2025 facility removed the one lever that breaks the circle.
Elections reset the clock
September 2025 turned out an incumbent on exactly these grievances. The new government inherits the same arithmetic and the same first year to spend political capital in.
Importers holding licences, connected traders, urban consumers facing a fuel price
Smallholder exporters, future factory workers, anyone not yet born
Concentrated, organised, voting now
Reform loses
Diffuse, unorganised, mostly silent

Widths are illustrative of political weight, not measured shares. The point is the asymmetry, which is the standard reason good economics loses to good politics.

This is the answer the professor is fishing for. "Why hasn't it happened" is almost never a knowledge problem. It is a distribution problem. Name who loses, and name why they can stop it.

12 What to say when called on

Sixty seconds, out loud

I looked at Malawi. Output per person is about $1,758 at PPP, roughly a thirty-eighth of Canada's, and it has fallen four years running because the economy grows 1.9% while the population grows 2.6%. The constraint that is actually binding is foreign exchange. Goods exports are $880 million, tobacco is 55% of that, and the kwacha has been held at 1,716 to the dollar since March 2024 while inflation ran at 28%. So dollars are rationed by permission rather than price, importers cannot buy fertiliser or fuel, and every other fix runs through that same missing dollar. What would start a transition is floating the currency, getting an IMF programme and a debt deal back, and moving the fertiliser subsidy into irrigation and export corridors. It has not happened because the reform takes a large concentrated income away from people who hold import permissions and gives a small diffuse gain to smallholders and workers who do not exist yet. That is a distribution problem, not a knowledge problem.

The three prep questions, answered

Question, as set
Pick a poor country, and do some research on it.
  1. What factors are constraining its growth?
  2. What would be needed to kickstart a transition for this country?
  3. Why hasn't that happened?
Part (a) · what is constraining growth

Lead with the binding one, then rank the rest. Foreign exchange is binding: $880m of goods exports against a $15bn economy, reserves under one month of cover, and an official rate frozen at 1,716 while prices rose 28.4%. Behind it sit five others: a single export crop at 55%, an 8.4% deficit crowding out credit, rain-fed agriculture holding 80% of workers, 15.6% grid access with 800 km to a port, and 2.6% population growth.

Growth per person = 1.9% − 2.6% = −0.7% a year

Then go one level down: the frozen rate is a policy choice that creates a rent, and that is the institutional cause behind the macro symptom. Worked in §8.

Part (b) · what would kickstart it

Sequence it, because the order is the answer. Float the kwacha with targeted cover for fuel and fertiliser. Use the credibility to restore an IMF facility, which lapsed in May 2025 without a review. Restructure the debt to reopen fiscal room. Then move the fertiliser subsidy into irrigation, the Nacala corridor and power.

Only then does a new export base become real: Kasiya rutile and graphite, higher value crops, or labour-intensive assembly. Doing the spending before the exchange rate is what the last twenty years were. Worked in §9.

Part (c) · why it hasn't happened

Because the losers are concentrated and the winners are diffuse. A float deletes the rent earned by anyone allocated dollars at the official rate, and raises fuel and maize prices within days. The gain, an export sector, arrives after the next election.

Two things keep the circle closed: aid at roughly 7% of GDP a year removes the deadline, and a float without reserves overshoots, so reform needs a programme that reform is the precondition for. September 2025 turned out an incumbent over exactly these grievances, and the arithmetic facing the new government is identical. Worked in §10.

Q&A log

Follow-up · September 21, 2026
Give me the reasons in bullet points.

Ordered deepest first. The further down the list you go, the more you are describing the symptom rather than the cause, which is exactly the mistake section 6 is built to catch.

Why rich places are rich

  • Property rights that hold. You invest because you keep the return. Everything else follows from this one
  • Contracts a stranger will honour. Lets firms grow past the family, which is the whole difference between a stall and a factory
  • Open entry. New firms can start, old ones can fail, and talent sorts by ability rather than connection
  • Power that is bound by the rules it sets. The state is strong enough to enforce and constrained enough not to loot
  • Openness to the outside. Trade, investment and imported know-how. Copying is far faster than inventing
  • Productivity, not stuff. The same worker with the same machine simply produces more, because the surrounding system works

Why poor places are poor

  • Rules built to extract. Income flows to whoever holds power, so investing is irrational and getting a permission is rational
  • Talent pointed at permissions. The able person applies for an import licence instead of building something
  • Prices set by decree. A fixed exchange rate or a controlled price creates a queue, and a queue creates a rent
  • Nothing to sell abroad. Thin exports mean no foreign currency, and no foreign currency means no imported inputs
  • Hostile geography, uncompensated. Landlocked, tropical, distant. Real, but Botswana and Switzerland show it is survivable
  • Too many workers in subsistence farming. No takeoff on record happened without moving people out of it
  • Population outrunning output. Malawi grows 1.9% with 2.6% more people, so living standards fall

Why poor places stay poor

  • The fix has concentrated losers. Reform deletes a large income for a few and gives a small gain to many. The few are organised
  • Costs land before benefits. Fuel and food prices move in days, an export sector takes a decade, elections take four years
  • Loops, not lines. No dollars, no fertiliser, worse harvest, fewer dollars. Nothing self-corrects
  • Aid removes the deadline. Enough to avoid collapse, never enough to force the change
  • Reform needs what reform creates. A float needs reserves, reserves need a programme, a programme needs the float
  • Resources make it worse, not better. Without contracts a discovery becomes something to fight over
The one-line version

Rich places are not rich because they have more. They are rich because the rules make producing worth more than capturing, and poor places stay poor because the people who capture are the people who would have to change the rules.

Asked in prep
If institutions are the answer, why did China grow 10% a year with bad ones?

Two replies, and use both. First, China's institutions changed enormously after 1978: household farming, private firms, land use rights, and entry into world trade. Judged against 1976 China they became far more inclusive, even though they never became democratic.

Second, catch-up growth and frontier growth need different things. Copying an existing technology needs order, savings and open trade, which an authoritarian state can supply. Inventing the next one needs entry, failure and contestable power. China grew fast on the first and the argument now is whether it can do the second.
