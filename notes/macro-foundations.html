The macro machine

In one lineThe economy has a speed limit (what it can produce) and a speed (what it is producing). The difference between them is the output gap, and that one number is what drives unemployment down, inflation up, and the Bank of Canada into the room.

The one sentence that makes the rest of the page make sense. Potential output moves slowly and is about supply — how many people, how much capital, how productive. Actual output moves fast and is about demand — how much everyone is spending. Long-run prosperity is a supply story. Booms, recessions and inflation are demand-versus-supply stories.

The three vital signs

Primer 01 calls this the dashboard. Three numbers describe the whole economy. Learn what each one is, what moves it, and — the part everyone skips — what its denominator is, because that is where the exam traps live.

Output

GDP

~$3T

Market value of all final goods and services produced inside Canada in a period. Grew ~1½–2% a year through the 2010s; worst peacetime collapse on record in early 2020.

Denominator trap: real vs nominal, and per capita vs total.

Jobs

Unemployment rate

~6½%

Share of the labour force that is looking for work and cannot find it. Spiked to 13.7% in May 2020, fell below 5% within two years, drifted back toward 7%, ~6½% by mid-2026.

Denominator trap: the labour force is not fixed. See P3.

Prices

CPI inflation

2% target

The rate of change of a fixed basket of consumer prices. Ran near 2% for two decades, hit 8.1% in June 2022 — highest in nearly forty years — and is back near target.

Denominator trap: it is a rate, not a level. See §4.

Figures from Primer 01 (Frost & Cornelson), which sources Statistics Canada and the Bank of Canada.

These three are not independent, and that is the whole course. Strong GDP pulls unemployment down → tight labour markets push wages up → wages push prices up → the Bank raises rates → spending falls → GDP slows. You can start anywhere on that chain; you always end up back at the start.

1 GDP — what “the economy” is a number of

In one lineGDP is one year’s worth of new stuff made inside the country, priced at what it sold for. Not wealth, not money, not how well anyone is doing — just production, over a period.

The formal definition is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country’s borders over a given period. Every word in it is doing work, and each word is a place an exam question hides.

The definition, word by word
WordWhat it rules in / outThe example that makes it stick
Market valueOnly things with a price. No price, no GDP.Pay a daycare → GDP rises. Stay home with your own kid → nothing, though the service is identical.
FinalIntermediate inputs are excluded to avoid double-counting.The bakery’s bread counts; the flour it bought does not — the flour is already inside the bread’s price.
ProducedMaking things, not trading things. Second-hand sales and financial trades are not production.Selling your used car adds ~nothing to GDP; the dealer’s commission does, because that is a service produced now.
Within a country’s bordersLocation, not ownership. That is GDP vs GNI.Toyota’s Ontario plant → Canada’s GDP. A Canadian miner’s Chilean pit → Chile’s GDP, Canada’s GNI.
Over a given periodA flow, not a stock. It has no memory.Your salary is a flow; your net worth is a stock. GDP is national salary, not national net worth.

Three ways to count the same thing

Production, expenditure and income are three cameras pointed at one circular flow. They must add to the same total — that forced agreement is the national accounts’ built-in audit.

Example — the loaf of bread, counted three ways
A farmer sells $0.40 of wheat to a miller. The miller sells $0.90 of flour to a baker. The baker sells the loaf for $3.00. Only $3.00 enters GDP, not $0.40 + $0.90 + $3.00 = $4.30.
Value added: farmer 0.40 + miller (0.90−0.40)=0.50 + baker (3.00−0.90)=2.10 = $3.00 ✓
Same $3.00 shows up as expenditure when you buy the loaf, and as income when it is split into the farmer’s, miller’s and baker’s wages and profits. Three cameras, one loaf.

The expenditure decomposition you will actually use

Ninety percent of GMM analysis runs through C + I + G + NX, because these four are the spending decisions that swing the business cycle.

C + I + G + NX — roughly, and what moves each
ComponentRough share of Canadian GDPWhat moves itCycle behaviour
C — household consumption~55%Income, jobs, confidence, mortgage ratesSmooth. Households defend their living standard.
I — business investment + housing + inventories~20%Interest rates, expected demand, policy uncertaintyViolent. This is where recessions are made.
G — government purchases~21%Budgets, politics, automatic stabilisersCounter-cyclical by design.
NX — exports minus importssmall net, huge grossU.S. demand, the exchange rate, tariffsCanada’s exposure. ~3/4 of exports go to the U.S.

Shares are approximate and shift; treat them as orders of magnitude, not quotables. Check StatCan Table 36-10-0104-01 for the current split. The point of the table is the behaviour column.

Why imports are subtracted. Not because imports are bad. Because C, I and G already include spending on foreign-made goods, and GDP only wants domestic production. Subtracting imports removes what was wrongly added. A country does not get poorer by importing — the minus sign is bookkeeping, not economics. This is the single most-misread line in the national accounts and it shows up in tariff arguments constantly.

What GDP deliberately does not see

Counted: anything with a price Imputed: owner-occupied rent, free chequing Excluded: housework, childcare, volunteering Excluded: consumer value of free digital services Mostly missed: the underground economy Not subtracted: depreciation, pollution, depletion

These are not moral judgements — they are measurement compromises made in the 1930s and 1940s, when Kuznets and his contemporaries “retreated to ground securely buttressed by reliable data.” GDP was invented, to count production and the capacity to mobilise it. It was never designed to measure welfare, and every criticism of it is downstream of that.

The four paradoxes worth being able to name in class
ParadoxWhat happensWhy GDP is not “wrong”
Disasters raise GDPRebuilding after Katrina or the 2011 Japanese tsunami adds to measured output.GDP is a flow of production, not a balance sheet. It records the repair truthfully and says nothing about the destruction. Bastiat’s broken window, 1850.
Free digital goodsSearch, maps and email cost you nothing; only the ad revenue enters GDP.Users pay in attention and data, not dollars. Brynjolfsson’s experimental GDP-B tries to price the consumer surplus — and finds it is enormous.

All four from Primer 04, GDP in Practice. Canada’s underground economy is estimated at ~2½% of GDP, roughly $72 billion in 2023, with residential construction the largest single piece.

2 Nominal vs real — and the deflator

In one lineIf the dollar value of everything you produced went up 10%, you do not know whether you made more stuff or charged more for the same stuff. Real GDP is the answer after you take the price change out.

Nominal GDP is measured at today’s prices and mixes two things together. Real GDP holds prices fixed and isolates the change in quantities. Whenever an economist says “growth”, they mean real. Whenever a newspaper says the economy is now worth $X, they mean nominal.

The three formulas — memorise these, they recur all term
GDP deflator = (Nominal GDP ÷ Real GDP) × 100
Real GDP = Nominal GDP ÷ (deflator ÷ 100)
(1 + nominal growth) = (1 + real growth) × (1 + inflation)
The deflator is not an input you look up — it is defined as the ratio. It is whatever number makes the two GDPs consistent.

Chain weighting, in one paragraph

The old method valued every year’s output at one fixed base year’s prices. That works while the economy changes slowly and breaks when it does not — the primer’s example is that a doubling in the price of horses meant something in 1930 and means nothing now. Chain weighting values each period at the immediately preceding period’s prices and links the growth rates together, so the weights stay current as new products arrive and old ones fade.

Chain weighting has one consequence that trips people up: chained real GDP components do not add up to chained real GDP. If you add real C + real I + real G + real NX from a StatCan table, you will not hit the real GDP line exactly. That is expected, not an error in your arithmetic.

Nominal GDP can boom while household purchasing power barely moves. This is the mechanism behind “the terms of trade”, which comes back later in the course.

3 CPI — the price of everything, in one number

In one lineStatistics Canada fills one imaginary shopping cart, freezes what is in it, and reprices that same cart every month. CPI is what the cart costs, indexed to 100 in a base year. Inflation is how fast the cart bill is climbing.

The fixed basket is the whole trick. If you let the contents change, you can no longer tell whether the bill went up because prices rose or because you bought different things. Freezing the cart isolates price. The cost of freezing it is that the basket slowly goes stale — which is why StatCan re-weights it every year or two.

Weights are the whole story

The basket is not one of each thing. Each category enters in proportion to how much households actually spend on it, so a 10% move in shelter matters roughly six times as much as a 10% move in clothing. Headline inflation is just a weighted average of category price changes — nothing more sophisticated is going on.

4 Inflation — the rate, not the level

In one lineInflation is how fast prices are rising, not how high they are. Inflation coming down means prices are still going up, just more slowly. Prices essentially never come back down — and that is why “we beat inflation” and “everything is unaffordable” are both true at once.

Why inflation is actually bad

“Prices go up but so do wages, so who cares?” is the natural objection, and the answer is that they do not go up together, or at the same time, or for the same people. Inflation is mostly a redistribution, and the losers did not agree to it.

Who loses

  • Wage earners — pay resets once a year; prices reset weekly. You are behind for eleven months out of twelve.
  • Savers and anyone on a fixed income — a pension not indexed to CPI loses real value every single month.
  • Lenders — they get repaid in dollars worth less than the ones they lent.
  • Everyone, through uncertainty — when the unit of account is unstable, long contracts and long investments get harder to write. This is the deep cost.

Who gains

  • Borrowers — a fixed mortgage shrinks in real terms while your nominal wage grows. Inflation is a silent transfer from lenders to debtors.
  • Governments — the largest debtors of all, and bracket creep raises real tax take on the side.
  • Owners of real assets — houses, land, commodities reprice upward with everything else.
  • Firms with pricing power — when everyone is raising prices, nobody notices yours.
Why the target is 2% and not 0%. Three reasons, and they come up every year. (1) Room to cut: if inflation sits at 2%, nominal rates sit around 4% in normal times, which gives the Bank something to cut in a recession. At 0% inflation, rates sit near zero and the Bank is out of ammunition. (2) Greasing wages: people will not accept a nominal pay cut, but they will accept a 1% raise when inflation is 2% — which is the same thing. A little inflation lets real wages adjust between shrinking and growing industries without anyone getting fired. (3) Deflation is worse: falling prices mean rising real debt, so households delay purchases and cut spending, which lowers prices further. Japan spent two decades in that trap. Aiming at 2% keeps a safe distance from it.
Example — the word that decides everything: anchored
Expectations are “anchored” when firms and workers keep assuming inflation will come back to 2%, even while it is printing 8%. Anchored means a supply shock stays a one-off. Un-anchored means wages get set at 6%, so costs rise 6%, so prices rise 6% — and the shock has become the new normal.
1970s: expectations un-anchored → Volcker needed a deep recession to re-anchor them
2022–24: expectations held → 8.1% → target in ~2 years, with no Canadian recession
That is the difference between the two great inflation episodes, and it is not mainly about the size of the shock. It is about whether anybody believed the central bank.

5 Unemployment — read the cascade, not the headline

In one lineNot everyone without a job is unemployed. You are only unemployed if you are looking. Stop looking and you vanish from the statistic entirely — which is why the rate can fall for terrible reasons and rise for good ones.

The four rates, and which denominator each uses
Unemployment rate = Unemployed ÷ Labour force = 1,436 ÷ 22,100 = 6.50%
Participation rate = Labour force ÷ Working-age = 22,100 ÷ 34,000 = 65.0%
Employment rate = Employed ÷ Working-age = 20,664 ÷ 34,000 = 60.8%
The employment rate is the tiebreaker, because it is the only one whose denominator cannot be gamed by people entering or leaving the search. When the unemployment rate and employment growth disagree, look here.

The three kinds, and why only one is the Bank’s problem

TypeWhat it isExampleFixed by
FrictionalThe normal churn of people between jobs.You quit in May and start in July.Nothing — and you would not want to. It is a sign of a fluid labour market.
StructuralThe jobs and the skills or locations do not match.A laid-off Windsor auto worker and an unfilled Calgary software role.Training, mobility, immigration policy. Not interest rates.
CyclicalThere is simply not enough demand.April 2020. Everyone laid off at once.Monetary and fiscal policy. This is the only one macro policy can touch.
NAIRU / the natural rate (u*). Frictional + structural unemployment never goes away, so “full employment” is not 0%. It is the rate at which inflation is stable — the non-accelerating-inflation rate of unemployment, somewhere around 6% in Canada. Push unemployment below u* and you are pulling workers out of a pool that has already run dry: wages accelerate, then prices. Unemployment below u* is the labour market’s version of a positive output gap, and that equivalence is the bridge to §6. Nobody knows u* exactly; it is estimated, revised, and argued about.
Box 1 from Primer 01, and P3 below: “the economy added jobs and the unemployment rate rose” is not a contradiction. It means the labour force grew faster than employment — students, discouraged workers returning, new immigrants. The reverse also happens: in early 2026 the Canadian rate dipped partly because fewer people were looking. Never read the rate alone. Pair it with employment growth, the participation rate, and hours worked.

6 The output gap — the concept the whole course hangs on

In one lineThe economy has a comfortable cruising speed. The output gap is how far above or below it you are actually going. Above: engine overheats, prices rise. Below: engine idles, people lose jobs.

Potential output is what the economy can produce with its workers, its capital and its technology, without generating rising inflation. It is set by supply and moves slowly — roughly 1½–2% a year in Canada. Actual output is what demand actually pulled out of it this quarter. The gap between them is a couple of percent at most, and it is the most consequential couple of percent in economics.

The formula — and the sign convention that gets marked wrong
Output gap (%) = (Actual real GDP − Potential real GDP) ÷ Potential real GDP × 100
Positive gap = running hot = inflationary = the Bank wants to slow you down.
Negative gap = slack = disinflationary = rising unemployment = the Bank wants to speed you up.
Potential is in the denominator, and potential is not observable — it is estimated. The Bank of Canada publishes a range, not a point, and revises it. Half the real disagreement between economists is a disagreement about where potential is.

7 Where GDP, unemployment and inflation join up

In one lineTwo rules of thumb chain the dashboard together. Okun connects the output gap to unemployment. Phillips connects unemployment to inflation. Put them end to end and one number — the gap — predicts the other two.

Okun’s law

u − u* = −½ × output gap

Every 2 percentage points of output gap moves unemployment about 1 point the other way. Not 1-for-1, because firms hoard labour — in a slowdown they first cut hours, overtime and hiring before they cut heads. That is why hours worked falls before employment does, and why hours is the better early indicator.

An empirical regularity, not a law. The coefficient drifts and differs across countries.

The Phillips curve

π = πe − κ(u − u*) + supply shock

Unemployment below u* means firms are bidding for scarce workers; wages rise, and wages are most firms’ largest cost. But the curve sits on top of expected inflation — πe is the whole curve’s height. Move expectations and you move every point on it at once, with no change in unemployment.

The original 1958 version had no πe term. That omission is what the 1970s destroyed.

Canadian and U.S. figures from Primer 01. Canadian CPI peaked at 8.1% in June 2022; the U.S. at 9.1%. The BoC policy rate settled at 2.25% by the mid-2020s.

Why the Phillips curve looked “dead” in the 2010s, then came back. From 2010 to 2019, unemployment fell a lot and inflation barely moved — so people declared the relationship broken. It was not. It was flat, because expectations were so firmly anchored at 2% that the πe term dominated everything else. When a shock big enough to move expectations finally arrived in 2021, the curve reappeared, steeply. Flat near the anchor, steep away from it — that non-linearity is the modern view, and it is a good answer to give in class.

8 Goods vs services — the split that decides the inflation story

In one lineGoods are things made in a factory, shipped, and priced globally. Services are someone’s time. Goods prices move fast in both directions; services prices move slowly and mostly one way — because services are wages, and wages are sticky.

Roughly 55–60% of Canadian household consumption is services and 40–45% goods, and the share of services has been climbing for a century. That split is not trivia. It explains the entire shape of the 2020–2026 inflation episode: why it arrived, why it fell so fast at first, and why the last two points took so long.

Example — the arithmetic that explains “the last mile”
Take a basket that is 45% goods and 55% services. Services inflation is stuck at 4%. What do goods have to do for headline to hit the 2% target?
0.45 × g + 0.55 × 4.0 = 2.0 0.45 × g = 2.0 − 2.20 = −0.20 g = −0.44% ← goods prices must actually FALL
That is the whole story of 2024–25 in one line. Headline inflation only looked calm because goods were in outright deflation, doing the work that services refused to do. It also tells you where the risk is: the moment goods stop falling — a tariff, a weaker loonie, a freight disruption — headline pops straight back up with no change in services at all.
Baumol’s cost disease — why services get relatively more expensive forever. A string quartet takes exactly as many musician-hours today as it did in 1826. A television takes a tiny fraction of the labour it took in 1970. But the musicians’ wages have to keep pace with economy-wide wages or they leave for other work. So the price of the quartet rises relative to the TV, permanently, with no inefficiency anywhere. This is why tuition, childcare, healthcare, restaurants and haircuts always feel like they are outrunning inflation. They are — and it is a mathematical consequence of productivity growth being concentrated in goods, not a scandal.
Canadian quirk worth knowing cold: mortgage interest cost is in our CPI. Unlike the U.S., Canada’s CPI shelter component includes mortgage interest cost. So when the Bank of Canada raised rates to fight inflation, it mechanically raised a chunk of measured CPI on the way. The cure temporarily inflated the disease. Through 2023–24 this was a meaningful contributor to headline — which is exactly why the Bank leans on CPI-trim and CPI-median rather than the headline number it is nominally targeting. If you can name this in class you are ahead of the room.

9 Productivity — the only thing that makes you permanently richer

In one lineProductivity is output per hour worked. You can grow GDP by adding people or adding hours, but that does not make anyone better off per person. Only producing more per hour does.

Krugman’s line is the one to remember: “Productivity isn’t everything, but in the long run it is almost everything.” Over a decade, the cycle washes out and the output gap averages zero. What is left is potential output, and potential output grows at the rate of labour force growth + productivity growth. Since you cannot raise the labour force growth rate forever, productivity is the entire long-run story — it is what Session 3’s “why are your grandchildren richer than you?” actually reduces to.

The three identities that carry all of it
Labour productivity = Real GDP ÷ Total hours worked
Real GDP growth = hours growth + productivity growth
Potential growth = labour force growth + productivity growth
These are identities, not theories — they are true by definition. Which makes them extremely useful: any GDP growth number can be split into “more work” and “better work”, and only the second one compounds into living standards.

What actually raises productivity

Capital deepening

More and better tools per worker. A ditch-digger with an excavator produces more per hour than one with a shovel, with no change in skill or effort.

Canada’s weak spot: business investment per worker has run well below the U.S. for years.

Human capital

Education, training, experience, and getting people into the jobs that use what they know.

Canada has among the highest post-secondary attainment in the OECD — and persistent credential-recognition problems that waste some of it.

Total factor productivity

Everything else: technology, management quality, competition, regulation, how efficiently capital and labour get combined. It is the residual — what growth is left after you account for more workers and more machines — and historically it is the biggest piece.

Canada’s productivity problem is a live, nameable issue. Output per hour has been roughly flat for years, and Canada produces meaningfully less per hour than the United States — the commonly cited gap is on the order of 25–30%. In a March 2024 speech, the Bank of Canada’s Senior Deputy Governor Carolyn Rogers called it an emergency and said it was “time to break the glass.” Usual suspects: thin business investment, small domestic market, interprovincial trade barriers, concentrated and sheltered industries, and slow diffusion of technology into mid-sized firms. Have a view on this; it is a guaranteed class discussion.

Directionally well established; check the current level gap in OECD GDP per hour worked or StatCan Table 36-10-0480-01 before quoting a precise number.

Why measured productivity understates the truth

Primer 04’s measurement chapter is really a chapter about productivity, because real GDP is nominal GDP divided by a price index — so every bias in measuring prices flows straight through into measured real growth, one for one. Overstate inflation and you understate productivity by exactly the same amount.

Five biases, all pointing the same way
BiasMechanismEffect on measured productivity
Matched-model / qualityA new car costs $45,000 vs $40,000 five years ago — but it is safer, more efficient, and better equipped. How much of the $5,000 is inflation?Too low

And note the irony that closes the loop with §8: the sectors that are hardest to measure (health, education, government, digital) are exactly the services whose share of the economy keeps rising. The part of the economy we measure worst is the part that is growing.

P1 · Building a CPI from scratch

The question, as set — self-set, exam-shaped
A statistical agency defines the consumer basket for a typical student. Quantities are fixed at the Year 0 level and do not change.
Item                    Qty/month     Price Yr 0     Price Yr 1
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Rent, one room               1          $900.00        $972.00
Groceries, per week          4           $95.00        $104.50
Transit pass                 1          $110.00        $115.00
Coffee                      20            $3.00          $3.30
Phone plan                   1           $50.00         $49.00
Required:
  1. Compute the cost of the basket in Year 0 and Year 1.
  2. Compute the CPI for Year 1, taking Year 0 = 100.
  3. Compute the rate of inflation between the two years.
  4. Compute each item’s expenditure weight in Year 0 and its contribution to inflation in percentage points. Verify the contributions sum to your answer in (c).
  5. Recompute inflation with groceries excluded. Explain why the result is not simply (c) minus groceries’ contribution.

Working it through

1

Price the same cart twice — quantities never move

This is the entire method. Every quantity is held at its Year 0 level in both columns, so any change in the bill is a change in prices, by construction. The trap is forgetting to multiply by quantity: groceries are four purchases a month, coffee is twenty.

Year 0 basket
  Rent           1 × $900.00  =  $  900.00
  Groceries      4 × $ 95.00  =  $  380.00
  Transit        1 × $110.00  =  $  110.00
  Coffee        20 × $  3.00  =  $   60.00
  Phone          1 × $ 50.00  =  $   50.00
                                 ──────────
  Total                         $1,500.00

Year 1 basket  — same quantities, new prices
  Rent           1 × $972.00  =  $  972.00
  Groceries      4 × $104.50  =  $  418.00
  Transit        1 × $115.00  =  $  115.00
  Coffee        20 × $  3.30  =  $   66.00
  Phone          1 × $ 49.00  =  $   49.00
                                 ──────────
  Total                         $1,620.00

The bill rose $120. That is the whole of (a).

2

Turn the two bills into an index

An index is just a ratio scaled so the base year reads 100. It carries no extra information — it exists so you can compare series with different dollar sizes.

CPI(Year 1) = (Year 1 basket cost ÷ Year 0 basket cost) × 100
            = ($1,620.00 ÷ $1,500.00) × 100
            = 1.08 × 100
            = 108.0      (CPI Year 0 = 100.0 by definition)
3

Index to inflation rate — the step everyone collapses

The index is a level; inflation is its rate of change. Here the base is 100, so the two look identical and you can get away with subtracting — which is exactly why people then do it in a year where the base is not 100 and get it wrong. Always divide.

Inflation = (CPI₁ − CPI₀) ÷ CPI₀ × 100
          = (108.0 − 100.0) ÷ 100.0 × 100
          = 8.0%

General form, for when the base is not 100:
  From 137.4 to 142.1  →  (142.1 − 137.4) ÷ 137.4 = 3.42%   not 4.7%
4

Extract the weights — they are just Year 0 spending shares

A weight is not a policy choice. It is simply how much of the base-year bill that item accounted for. Note how far rent dominates: 60% of the basket, so nothing else can move headline much on its own.

Weight = item's Year 0 spend ÷ total Year 0 spend

  Rent        900 ÷ 1,500  =  60.000%
  Groceries   380 ÷ 1,500  =  25.333%
  Transit     110 ÷ 1,500  =   7.333%
  Coffee       60 ÷ 1,500  =   4.000%
  Phone        50 ÷ 1,500  =   3.333%
                              ────────
                             100.000%  ✓ sums to 100
5

Contribution = weight × that item’s own price change

Headline inflation is a weighted average, nothing more. Contributions must cross-foot to the headline — that check catches almost every arithmetic error you can make here. Note the phone plan contributing negatively: falling prices in one category genuinely subtract from headline.

Own price change:
  Rent        972.00 ÷ 900.00 − 1  =  + 8.000%
  Groceries   104.50 ÷  95.00 − 1  =  +10.000%
  Transit     115.00 ÷ 110.00 − 1  =  + 4.545%
  Coffee        3.30 ÷   3.00 − 1  =  +10.000%
  Phone        49.00 ÷  50.00 − 1  =  − 2.000%

Contribution = weight × own price change:
  Rent        0.60000 × ( 8.000)  =  +4.8000 pp
  Groceries   0.25333 × (10.000)  =  +2.5333 pp
  Transit     0.07333 × ( 4.545)  =  +0.3333 pp
  Coffee      0.04000 × (10.000)  =  +0.4000 pp
  Phone       0.03333 × (−2.000)  =  −0.0667 pp
                                     ──────────
  Headline                          +8.0000 pp  ✓ = step 3

Fast sanity route: each contribution is just that item’s dollar change over the total base bill. Rent = $72/$1,500 = 4.8pp. Same answer, no percentages needed.

6

Strip food out — and watch the weights rescale

This is the part (e) trap, and it is a real one that appears in every “headline vs core” discussion. When you delete a category you delete it from the denominator too, so every surviving weight gets bigger. Do it both ways to prove it.

Route 1 — reprice the smaller basket directly
  Year 0 ex-groceries:  1,500.00 − 380.00  =  $1,120.00
  Year 1 ex-groceries:  1,620.00 − 418.00  =  $1,202.00
  Inflation = (1,202 ÷ 1,120) − 1 = 82 ÷ 1,120 = 7.321%

Route 2 — rescale the surviving contributions
  Contributions excl. groceries:
    4.8000 + 0.3333 + 0.4000 − 0.0667  =  5.4667 pp
  Rescale by the lost weight:
    5.4667 × (1,500 ÷ 1,120)  =  5.4667 × 1.33929  =  7.321%  ✓ both routes agree

The naive answer:  8.000 − 2.533 = 5.467%   ✗ wrong — forgot to rescale

Why it matters: groceries rose 10% against a headline of 8%, so removing them should lower the answer — and it does, to 7.3%, not to 5.5%. The 1.85pp difference is pure denominator effect. Say “contribution” when you mean contribution and “ex-food inflation” when you mean the recomputed index; they are different objects.

P2 · Nominal → real → the deflator

The question, as set — self-set, exam-shaped
An economy produces exactly two things: bread (a good) and haircuts (a service).
                    Year 1                      Year 2
              Quantity     Price         Quantity     Price
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Bread            100       $ 3.00           104       $ 3.30
Haircuts          50       $20.00            52       $22.00
Required:
  1. Nominal GDP in each year, and the rate of nominal GDP growth.
  2. Real GDP in Year 2 measured at Year 1 prices, and the rate of real GDP growth.
  3. The GDP deflator for Year 2 with Year 1 = 100, and the implied rate of inflation.
  4. Show that nominal growth is not exactly real growth plus inflation, and state when the shortcut is safe.
  5. Would the Bank of Canada target this deflator? Explain in two sentences why CPI inflation could differ.

Working it through

1

Nominal GDP: this year’s quantities at this year’s prices

Nominal means “as it actually happened, in the dollars of the day.” Both the quantity column and the price column are allowed to move, which is exactly why the answer is uninterpretable on its own.

Year 1 nominal
  Bread      100 × $ 3.00  =  $  300.00
  Haircuts    50 × $20.00  =  $1,000.00
                               ──────────
                              $1,300.00

Year 2 nominal
  Bread      104 × $ 3.30  =  $  343.20
  Haircuts    52 × $22.00  =  $1,144.00
                               ──────────
                              $1,487.20

Nominal growth = (1,487.20 ÷ 1,300.00) − 1 = 187.20 ÷ 1,300 = +14.40%

A 14.4% headline. Now find out how much of it was real.

2

Real GDP: this year’s quantities at last year’s prices

Freeze the price column at Year 1 and let only quantities move. What is left is pure production. The trap is freezing the wrong column — if you hold quantities and move prices you have built a price index, not real GDP.

Year 2 real, at Year 1 prices
  Bread      104 × $ 3.00  =  $  312.00   ← Year 1 price
  Haircuts    52 × $20.00  =  $1,040.00   ← Year 1 price
                               ──────────
                              $1,352.00

Real growth = (1,352.00 ÷ 1,300.00) − 1 = 52.00 ÷ 1,300 = +4.00%

So of a 14.4% nominal headline, only 4.0% was more bread and more haircuts. The other ten points were price.

3

The deflator is not looked up — it is whatever reconciles the two

Define it as the ratio and it cannot be wrong. It is the economy-wide price index implied by the accounts.

Deflator(Yr 2) = (Nominal ÷ Real) × 100
               = (1,487.20 ÷ 1,352.00) × 100
               = 1.10000 × 100
               = 110.0        (Year 1 = 100 by construction)

Deflator inflation = (110.0 − 100.0) ÷ 100.0 = +10.0%
4

The two components multiply, they do not add

Prices rose on the larger quantity, so there is a cross term. At 2% inflation the shortcut is harmless; at 2022’s 8% it is not.

Shortcut:   4.0% + 10.0%                    =  14.00%   ✗ off by 0.40pp
Exact:      (1 + 0.04) × (1 + 0.10) − 1
            = 1.04 × 1.10 − 1
            = 1.1440 − 1                    =  14.40%   ✓ matches step 1

The cross term:  0.04 × 0.10 = 0.0040 = 0.40pp

Rule: below roughly 3% on both sides, add and move on. Above that, multiply — and always multiply when compounding over several years.

5

Which index the Bank targets, and why they diverge

The Bank of Canada targets CPI inflation at 2%, not the deflator. Two reasons: CPI is what households actually face, and it is the index pensions, wages and contracts are indexed to.

Deflator covers what the country PRODUCES  →  includes exports, excludes imports
CPI      covers what households BUY        →  includes imports, excludes exports

So in Canada an oil price spike raises the deflator sharply (crude is a large export) while barely touching CPI directly. The two indexes can print meaningfully different numbers in the same quarter without either being wrong.

P3 · “Jobs added, unemployment up”

The question, as set — self-set, exam-shaped · the Primer 01 Box 1 puzzle, with numbers
Two consecutive monthly Labour Force Survey releases, in thousands. Assume the working-age population is unchanged between the two months.
                                  Month 1      Month 2
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Working-age population (15+)       34,000       34,000
Labour force                       22,100       22,160
Employed                           20,664       20,689
Required:
  1. Compute the unemployment rate, participation rate and employment rate for each month.
  2. How many jobs were added?
  3. A newspaper runs the headline “Economy adds 25,000 jobs — unemployment rate climbs to 6.6%.” Explain how both halves can be true.
  4. Did the labour market get stronger or weaker? Name the single number that settles it, and state the assumption your answer depends on.

Working it through

1

Back out the unemployed — it is never given directly

The survey reports the labour force and employment. Unemployment is the residual, and everything else follows from it.

Unemployed = Labour force − Employed

  Month 1:  22,100 − 20,664  =  1,436
  Month 2:  22,160 − 20,689  =  1,471       (+35)
2

Three rates, three different denominators — this is the whole question

Write the denominator out every time. Unemployment divides by the labour force; the other two divide by the working-age population. Getting these crossed is the most common error in the whole topic.

                        Month 1                    Month 2
Unemployment rate   1,436 ÷ 22,100  = 6.50%    1,471 ÷ 22,160  = 6.64%
Participation rate 22,100 ÷ 34,000  = 65.00%  22,160 ÷ 34,000  = 65.18%
Employment rate    20,664 ÷ 34,000  = 60.78%  20,689 ÷ 34,000  = 60.85%

Every single one of those three moved up. Two of those moves are good news and one is bad news — which is the puzzle.

3

Jobs added, and where the new entrants went

Now decompose the 60,000 people who joined the labour force. That decomposition is the answer to (c).

Jobs added        = 20,689 − 20,664  =  +25 thousand
Labour force grew = 22,160 − 22,100  =  +60 thousand
                                         ──────
Entrants who did not find work immediately =  +35 thousand

  ✓ and indeed unemployed rose 1,436 → 1,471, exactly +35

The mechanism, in one sentence: the numerator of the unemployment rate rose by 35 while the denominator rose by 60, and 35/60 is far above the existing 6.5% rate, so the ratio had to climb. The rate went up because more people started looking, not because anyone was laid off — not one job was lost in this dataset.

4

Which number settles it

The employment rate, because it is the only one of the three whose denominator does not move when people start or stop searching. It rose from 60.78% to 60.85%: a larger share of all working-age Canadians is in a job than last month. On that test the labour market got stronger.

Employment rate  60.78%  →  60.85%      +0.07pp — genuinely more people working
Participation    65.00%  →  65.18%      +0.18pp — people re-entering, a confidence signal
Unemployment      6.50%  →   6.64%      +0.14pp — a denominator artefact here

The assumption this rests on: the question freezes the working-age population. In reality it grows every month through immigration and aging-in, and in Canada recently it has grown fast. If working-age population had also risen 60 thousand, the employment rate would have been 20,689 ÷ 34,060 = 60.74% — a fall, and the opposite verdict. Always check whether the denominators are moving before you call the direction.

P4 · Rebuilding June 2022 from three assumptions

The question, as set — self-set, exam-shaped · chains §6 and §7 together
An economy is described by the standard rules of thumb: Okun with a coefficient of ½, and an expectations-augmented Phillips curve with slope κ = 1.0.
Potential real GDP, annualised        $2,600.0 billion
Actual real GDP, annualised           $2,657.2 billion
NAIRU (u*)                                   6.0%
Expected inflation (πᵉ)                      3.0%
Supply shock (energy, food, freight)        +4.0 pp

  u − u*  =  −0.5 × output gap
  π       =  πᵉ  −  κ(u − u*)  +  supply shock
Required:
  1. Compute the output gap.
  2. Compute the unemployment rate implied by Okun’s law.
  3. Compute the inflation rate implied by the Phillips curve.
  4. Decompose your answer to (c) into its demand, supply and expectations pieces.
  5. Recompute (c) if expectations had stayed anchored at 2.0%, and if they had drifted to 5.0%. Comment.
  6. The supply shock now fades to zero but expectations remain at 3.0%. What output gap and unemployment rate are needed to return inflation to 2%?

Working it through

1

The gap — and note what sits in the denominator

Potential, not actual, goes on the bottom. The gap is expressed as a percentage of capacity, which is what makes it comparable across countries and decades.

Output gap = (Actual − Potential) ÷ Potential × 100
           = (2,657.2 − 2,600.0) ÷ 2,600.0 × 100
           = 57.2 ÷ 2,600.0 × 100
           = +2.20%      positive → the economy is running hot

Two-point-two percent sounds tiny. It is not: an economy running 2.2% above its sustainable capacity for a year is an economy where every plant is at full tilt, every posting has three unfilled seats, and every supplier has pricing power.

2

Okun — translate the gap into the labour market

Watch the sign. A positive gap drives unemployment below u*, so the minus sign in the formula is doing real work. Half, not one-for-one, because firms hoard labour: they add overtime and hours before they add heads.

u − u* = −0.5 × output gap
       = −0.5 × (+2.20)
       = −1.10 pp

u = u* + (u − u*)
  = 6.0 − 1.10
  = 4.90%      1.1pp below the natural rate — a genuinely tight labour market

Canada’s unemployment rate did in fact print around 4.9–5.0% in mid-2022, the tightest on record at that point. The model is not being flattered here.

3

Phillips — translate the labour market into prices

Substitute carefully: (u − u*) is negative, and it is being subtracted, so the term comes out positive. Two sign flips in one line is where this question is won or lost.

π = πᵉ − κ(u − u*) + supply shock
  = 3.0 − 1.0 × (−1.10) + 4.0
  = 3.0 + 1.10 + 4.0
  = 8.10%

Canadian CPI peaked at 8.1% in June 2022. Three assumptions and two rules of thumb land on the actual print — which is the point of the exercise, not a coincidence to be proud of.

4

Decompose it — this is the part that earns marks

Anyone can compute 8.1%. The analytical move is splitting it into pieces a policymaker can and cannot act on.

  Target / baseline expectation            2.00 pp    where πᵉ should have been
  Expectations drift  (3.0 − 2.0)          1.00 pp    credibility slipping
  Demand pressure     −κ(u − u*)           1.10 pp    the output gap
  Supply shock        energy, food, freight 4.00 pp   outside the Bank's reach
                                          ────────
  Total inflation                          8.10 pp   ✓ cross-foots to step 3

  Bank of Canada can act on:  1.00 + 1.10  =  2.10 pp   26% of it
  Bank of Canada cannot:                      4.00 pp   49% of it

What this tells you: roughly half the inflation was a supply shock that rate hikes are useless against — which is the honest case for the “transitory” view. The counter-case is the 1.00pp of drift, because drift compounds: this year’s realised inflation becomes next year’s expectation, and then the shock has become permanent. That is the entire 2021–22 debate, in two numbers.

5

Hold everything else fixed and move only expectations

Same output gap, same shock, same labour market. Only the number in people’s heads changes.

πᵉ = 2.0  (anchored)   →  π = 2.0 + 1.10 + 4.0  =   7.10%
πᵉ = 3.0  (drifting)   →  π = 3.0 + 1.10 + 4.0  =   8.10%
πᵉ = 5.0  (un-anchored)→  π = 5.0 + 1.10 + 4.0  =  10.10%

Span attributable to expectations alone:  3.00 pp
Span attributable to the entire output gap: 1.10 pp   expectations are ~3× as powerful

Comment: a variable no statistical agency measures, that exists only as a belief, moves inflation three times as much as the actual state of the economy. This is why central bank communication is not public relations — it is the instrument. It is also why the Bank hiked from 0.25% to 5.00% in sixteen months and said loudly that it would keep going: the visible aggression was aimed at πe, not directly at the output gap.

6

The bill for letting expectations slip — the sacrifice ratio

The shock has gone, but πe is still 3.0%. Solve backwards for the gap that delivers 2%.

Substitute Okun into Phillips to get π in terms of the gap directly:
  π = πᵉ − κ(−0.5 × gap) + shock  =  πᵉ + 0.5 × gap + shock

Set π = 2.0, πᵉ = 3.0, shock = 0:
  2.0 = 3.0 + 0.5 × gap
  0.5 × gap = −1.0
  gap = −2.00%      the economy must run 2% BELOW capacity

And the unemployment that implies:
  u − u* = −0.5 × (−2.00) = +1.00 pp
  u = 6.0 + 1.00 = 7.00%

That is the cost of the 1.00pp of drift: a deliberately weak economy and unemployment at 7% until the belief resets. Primer 01 records exactly this — Canadian unemployment drifting “up toward 7 percent as the economy cooled under higher interest rates.” The recession is not the punishment for the supply shock. It is the punishment for the expectations drift — and that is why keeping πe anchored is worth so much.

The answers

(a) Output gap+2.20%
(b) Unemployment (Okun)4.90%
(c) Inflation (Phillips)8.10%
(d) Decomposition — baseline / drift / demand / supply2.00 / 1.00 / 1.10 / 4.00 pp
(e) Inflation if πe = 2.0 / 3.0 / 5.07.10 / 8.10 / 10.10 %
(f) Gap and unemployment needed to return to 2%−2.00%  ·  u = 7.00%
Coefficients are stylised. The real Bank of Canada uses a large structural model, publishes potential output as a range, and revises it. But every one of its Monetary Policy Reports is telling this exact story in this exact order: where is the gap, what is it doing to the labour market, what is that doing to prices, and are expectations holding.

P5 · Real interest rates and real wages

The question, as set — self-set, exam-shaped
Selected Canadian figures. Use the Fisher relation, exactly rather than approximately, and report both.
                                  2021        2023
────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Bank of Canada policy rate         0.25%       5.00%
CPI inflation                      4.80%       4.00%

June 2022:   CPI inflation 8.1%,  average hourly wage growth 5.2%
A 5-year fixed mortgage taken in 2021 at a nominal rate of 2.0%,
with inflation averaging 4.0% over its term.
Required:
  1. Compute the real policy rate in 2021 and in 2023, approximately and exactly.
  2. Compute the change in the real wage in June 2022.
  3. Compute the real interest rate on the mortgage, and the cumulative real erosion of that debt over five years.
  4. In two sentences, state who inflation transferred wealth from and to.

Working it through

1

Fisher — the real rate is what is left after inflation eats

Nominal is the number on the contract. Real is what it is worth in stuff. The approximation subtracts; the exact version divides, because the interest is earned on dollars that are themselves shrinking.

Approximation:  real ≈ nominal − inflation
Exact (Fisher): (1 + real) = (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation)

2021
  Approx:  0.25 − 4.80                =  −4.55%
  Exact:   (1.0025 ÷ 1.0480) − 1
         = 0.95658 − 1                =  −4.34%

2023
  Approx:  5.00 − 4.00                =  +1.00%
  Exact:   (1.0500 ÷ 1.0400) − 1
         = 1.00962 − 1                =  +0.96%

What the sign means: in 2021 the real rate was deeply negative — you were paid roughly 4.3% a year, in purchasing power, to borrow. Every incentive in the economy pointed at spending and borrowing now. That is not a side effect of the 2021 policy stance; it was the policy stance, and it is a large part of why the demand-side gap in P4 opened up.

2

The raise that was a pay cut

Same arithmetic, different contract. Divide the wage index by the price index.

Real wage change = (1 + wage growth) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1
                 = (1.052 ÷ 1.081) − 1
                 = 0.97317 − 1
                 = −2.68%       approx: 5.2 − 8.1 = −2.9pp

A 5.2% raise — the largest most people had seen in a decade — and purchasing power fell 2.7%. This is the honest reply to “wages go up too, so inflation doesn’t matter.” Wages reset once a year; prices reset weekly. The lag is the loss, and it falls on employees, not employers.

3

The mortgage that shrank on its own

Real rate = (1.020 ÷ 1.040) − 1
          = 0.98077 − 1
          = −1.92% per year

Cumulative over 5 years:
  (1.020 ÷ 1.040)⁵ − 1  =  (0.980769)⁵ − 1
                        =  0.90748 − 1
                        =  −9.25%

Before repaying a single dollar of principal, the real burden of that debt fell by more than nine percent, purely because the dollars used to repay it are worth less. The lender absorbed that loss. Nobody signed a contract agreeing to this transfer — it happened because inflation came in above what both parties priced in when the rate was set. That is the definition of unexpected inflation, and it is why the unexpected part is what does the damage.

4

The two-sentence answer

Inflation transferred wealth from lenders, savers, people on fixed or slow-adjusting incomes, and anyone holding nominal assets — to borrowers with fixed-rate debt, owners of real assets such as housing and commodities, governments servicing nominal debt, and firms with the pricing power to raise prices faster than their costs rose. Because the transfer is driven by the unexpected component, it lands hardest on whoever was least able to see it coming or least able to reprice — which is systematically the households at the bottom, not the institutions at the top.

P6 · Growth without getting richer

The question, as set — self-set, exam-shaped 
                                  Year A        Year B
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Real GDP ($ billions)             2,500.0       2,548.0
Total hours worked (millions)    33,300.0      34,000.0
Population (millions)                40.0          41.0
Required:
  1. Compute labour productivity (real GDP per hour worked) in each year.
  2. Compute the growth rates of real GDP, hours worked and labour productivity, and verify the identity linking them.
  3. Compute real GDP per capita in each year and its growth rate.
  4. Explain to a non-economist how the economy grew while the average person got poorer.
  5. Potential output growth equals labour force growth plus productivity growth. If the labour force grows 1.8%, what is potential growth? What productivity growth would be needed for potential growth of 2.5%?

Working it through

1

Get the units right before touching a calculator

Billions of dollars over millions of hours. Convert GDP to millions so the units cancel into dollars per hour, otherwise you will be out by a factor of a thousand and not notice.

Year A:  $2,500.0 bn = $2,500,000 mn
         2,500,000 ÷ 33,300  =  $75.08 per hour

Year B:  $2,548.0 bn = $2,548,000 mn
         2,548,000 ÷ 34,000  =  $74.94 per hour

Sanity check: an advanced economy produces on the order of $70–90 of output per hour worked. If you got $75,000 or $0.075, you dropped a conversion.

2

Three growth rates, and the identity that binds them

Real GDP growth   = (2,548.0 ÷ 2,500.0) − 1  =  48.0 ÷ 2,500  =  +1.920%
Hours growth      = (34,000 ÷ 33,300)  − 1   =  700 ÷ 33,300  =  +2.102%
Productivity      = (74.94 ÷ 75.08)    − 1                    =  −0.178%

Verify the identity  (1+GDP) = (1+hours) × (1+productivity):
  1.02102 × 0.99822  =  1.01920   ✓ matches GDP growth exactly

Approximation:  1.920 − 2.102 = −0.182   close enough at these rates

Read the signs. Output rose. Hours rose more. So every hour worked produced slightly less than the year before. The economy is bigger and less efficient at the same time — growth bought entirely with labour input.

3

Per capita — the number that actually describes a life

Year A:  2,500.0 bn ÷ 40.0 mn  =  $62,500 per person
Year B:  2,548.0 bn ÷ 41.0 mn  =  $62,146 per person

Growth  = (62,146 ÷ 62,500) − 1  =  −0.566%

Cross-check against population growth:
  Population growth = (41.0 ÷ 40.0) − 1 = +2.500%
  1.01920 ÷ 1.02500 = 0.99434  →  −0.566%   ✓ same answer, two routes
4

The non-economist version

Think of it as a pizza restaurant. Last year it made 2,500 pizzas with 100 staff. This year it made 2,548 pizzas — more pizza, the owner announces record output — but it hired 102 staff to do it, and there are now 105 people expecting a slice instead of 100. Each cook produced slightly fewer pizzas per shift, and each person at the table got a smaller piece.

Bigger economy       +1.92%   ← headline, true
More hours worked    +2.10%   ← where all of it came from
Output per hour      −0.18%   ← the economy got slightly worse at its job
More people to share +2.50%   ← the denominator
                     ───────
Slice per person     −0.57%   ← what anyone actually experiences

The general rule worth carrying out of this course: growth from more input raises GDP and nothing else. Growth from higher productivity is the only kind that raises living standards, and it is the only kind that compounds — because you can only add hours until everyone is working, whereas output per hour has no ceiling.

5

What the speed limit is set to

Potential growth = labour force growth + productivity growth
                 = 1.80% + (−0.178%)
                 = 1.62%

For potential growth of 2.5% with the labour force at 1.8%:
  (1 + 0.025) ÷ (1 + 0.018) − 1  =  1.025 ÷ 1.018 − 1  =  +0.69%
  (the additive shortcut gives 2.5 − 1.8 = 0.70% — fine here)

Required swing in productivity growth:  −0.18%  →  +0.69%  =  0.87pp

Note what this rules out. You cannot reach 2.5% potential growth by adding more people indefinitely — immigration raises both the numerator and the population, so it lifts GDP without lifting GDP per capita. You reach it by moving that second term, which means investment, technology and better use of the skills already here. This is exactly why the Bank of Canada called weak productivity an emergency, and it is the same argument as Session 3’s “will the next generation be richer?” — that question is asking whether this one number stays positive for thirty years.

Headwinds: U.S. tariff pressure and CUSMA uncertainty; flat productivity; high household debt with renewals repricing upward; concentrated export exposure (roughly three-quarters of exports go to one customer). 2 · Anchor each scenario in the framework on this page rather than in vibes. Session 3 · Sept 16 · Economic Prosperity and Progress
As set on Canvas
“Do you believe the next generation will be ‘richer’ than yours? Why or why not?”
Yes on consumption, no on the things people actually mean by rich — and the gap between those two answers is the interesting part.

The case for yes is arithmetic. Real GDP per capita has compounded for two centuries through wars, depressions and two once-in-a-generation crises, and the measured series almost certainly understates the gain, because quality improvement, new goods and free digital services are systematically missed. A generation that grows up with AI tools and treatments that do not exist today will consume something the current statistics cannot price.

The case for no is that Canada’s growth has lately come from more people working more hours rather than from each hour producing more — and only the second one compounds. Flat productivity plus an aging population is a recipe for GDP that grows while GDP per capita does not. Layer on the two components where the next generation is genuinely behind — housing costs relative to income, and the climate liability nobody is subtracting from the national accounts — and “richer in consumption, poorer in security” is a coherent position.

My actual answer: yes, but by less than history would predict, and unevenly — and whether I am right turns on one variable, productivity growth, which is exactly the one Canada is currently failing at.
The move that makes this an A answer
Do not argue about optimism. Convert the question into a claim about a measurable variable, then say what would change your mind. Here: “this is a forecast of productivity growth over thirty years, and here is the number I would watch.” Matt Yglesias’ assigned piece, Nostalgia Economics is Totally Wrong, is doing exactly this — he wins by dragging a vibes argument onto measurable ground.

My definition of rich is median consumption plus time plus security. GDP per capita is an excellent proxy for the first term, silent on the second and third, and — being a mean — actively misleading about the first in an unequal society.

But the right conclusion is not that GDP is the wrong measure. It is that GDP is the right measure of what it measures. It was invented in the 1930s to count production and the capacity to mobilise it, and it does that better than any alternative anyone has built. The mistake is not using GDP; it is using GDP alone. Read it next to median income, the employment rate, life expectancy and Adjusted Net Savings, and you have something close to what I mean by rich.
Have one of the beyond-GDP measures from Primer 04’s appendix ready by name — HDI, the OECD Better Life Index, Adjusted Net Savings, or SEEA. Naming a specific alternative and what it adds is worth more than a general complaint that GDP is incomplete.

Q1 · So how does raising an interest rate actually lower the price of groceries?

It does not, directly — and that is the honest answer. The policy rate is the rate banks lend to each other overnight. Nothing about it touches a tomato. It works through five channels, all of which reduce someone’s spending, and the whole chain takes six to eight quarters.

Example — why the lag makes this hard
A hike in March 2022 does not bite a household until their mortgage renews — possibly in 2027. Firms finish projects already underway. So the Bank is always steering a car whose wheel responds a year and a half later, using an estimate of potential output it cannot observe, against a shock it cannot see the end of.
This is why central banks overshoot in both directions — and why “they should have moved sooner” is always available with hindsight.
It also explains the sequencing in §8: channel 3 (the exchange rate, hitting imported goods) works in months, while channels 1, 2 and 4 reach services inflation only through the labour market, over years. Goods disinflate first. Services take the long road.
