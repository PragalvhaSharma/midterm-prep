Concepts

6 sections · 1 interactive

1 Canada's stall

In one lineCanadian firms stopped buying the tools that make workers more productive, and the gap with the US widened.

The op-ed's diagnosis: Canada's problem is not a shortage of inventions. It is falling behind on building capital (1i) and has barely started putting the world's best technology to work (2i).

Example · what the investment gap means
For every $10,000 a US firm invests per worker, a Canadian firm now invests about $5,400. Ten years ago it was $8,700.
8,700 → 5,400 = a 38% fall relative to the US

Income bands are the World Bank's (Session 6). Canada's GDP per person is an approximate IMF 2024 figure, not from the reading. The research labs are context, not from the op-ed.
Example · the evidence for "adoption matters most"
Across 15 major technologies and 166 countries over two centuries, countries adopted new technologies 45 years after invention on average. Differences in adoption speed explain at least a quarter of income gaps.

3 Breadth vs depth

In one line"Using AI" is cheap; rebuilding how work gets done is not. Canada has nearly caught the US on the first and barely started the second.

Shallow (breadth)

Draft emails, summarize documents, analyze a spreadsheet. A $30-a-month subscription.

Counts toward 60%

Deep (depth)

Machine shop predicting equipment failure. Insurer settling claims in minutes. Clinic doctors no longer typing notes.

What actually lifts output
The 19% (StatCan) and 8% (Bank of Canada) come from different surveys with different questions. Don't say "11% are shallow users." Say depth is well under half of breadth.

4 The J-curve

In one lineDoing AI properly makes a firm worse before better, while it retrains, redesigns jobs and fixes its data.

Evidence: a Bank of Korea study of 14,000+ Korean firms found no measurable gain for at least five years after starting with AI. That is why small firms with thin margins never start.

Example · why compute misses
A 12-person HVAC contractor will use AI through scheduling software it rents. Free GPU hours do nothing for it. A consultant who redesigns its dispatch process does.
Regulatory lag: no AI-specific law (the last bill died in 2025). The federal banking regulator's AI model guideline only takes effect May 2027. Until then, nobody knows who is liable when AI gets it wrong.

Electricity paid off when factories were rebuilt around it, not when it was invented. Canada does not own a frontier lab, so the rents from models flow to US firms either way. The gains we can capture are in use.

Bar length = my judgment of how hard each critique lands. Illustrative, not data.

The critic's five strongest points

1

Turn the op-ed's own framework against it

The World Bank sequence says once you're rich, you innovate. Korea escaped by moving to 3i: Samsung, about 5% of GDP on R&D. Prescribing 2i to a high-income country runs the ladder backwards.

2

Absorptive capacity

Firms adopt well only if they have people who understand the frontier. Cut frontier ambition and you lose the experts who make deep adoption possible.

3

Winner-take-most economics

AI value may pool in a few model owners. A pure adopter pays rent to US firms forever. Unlike electricity, the tech isn't a cheap commodity yet.

4

Sovereignty, after tariffs and CUSMA

2026 shows the cost of depending on the US. Health, defence and government data on US models is a strategic risk.

5

Adoption may not need policy at all

Breadth tripled in two years with no strategy. Firms adopt when it pays. The market failure is research, a public good, so that's where public money belongs.

Your rebuttal in one line: the op-ed doesn't abandon the frontier ("deserves continued support"). It argues the marginal dollar earns more in adoption, and Canada's gap is in use, not ideas.

Cold-call drill

Canvas says expect individual follow-ups, oral-exam style. Hide the answers, say yours out loud, then reveal.

Follow-up 1 · the premise
Likely question
Canada is one of the richest countries on earth. Isn't calling it "middle income" just wrong?
On income, yes. But the op-ed uses it as a diagnosis, not a label. The trap is about growth engines: our investment engine is fading (54¢ per US dollar) and adoption barely started. That pattern, not the income level, is what matches.
Follow-up 2 · the target
Likely question
Isn't breadth a fine first step? You have to start somewhere.
Breadth is fine as a step, bad as a target. It already tripled to 19% on its own. A 60% goal rewards chatbot use, and the productivity gap stays open. Target what you want: core-process use and output per worker.
Follow-up 3 · competition
Likely question
Why is competition policy an AI policy?
Rebuilding a core process is painful. A big bank with no rival threatening its customers has no reason to endure it. Competition creates the reason. It's also free, unlike $2.3 billion in programs.
Follow-up 4 · the J-curve
Likely question
If gains take five years, how would a government know in time that its strategy is working?
Track leading indicators: investment in software, data and training per worker, and core-process adoption. Then confirm with lagging ones: output per worker in adopting firms. Expect the dip; judge on the trend.
Follow-up 5 · your own view
Likely question
What would change your mind?
Evidence that deep adopters aren't more productive after the dip, or that countries without frontier research absorb AI badly. Either would push me from 4 toward 3.
