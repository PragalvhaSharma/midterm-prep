What that chart actually is

In one lineIt is the production approach to GDP, drawn as a donut. Every slice is one industry’s value added — what it sold, minus what it bought from other firms — and the slices add to the whole economy because every dollar of output belongs to exactly one industry.

Primer 03 gives three ways to measure the same total: production, expenditure, and income. The expenditure one (C + I + G + NX) is what you see in most macro coverage. This tool is the production one — sum the value added at every stage, by industry. Same $2.36 trillion, different camera angle.

Why value added and not revenue. If you summed every industry’s sales you would count the same steel four times — once at the mill, again in the part, again in the car, again at the dealership. Value added strips out what each industry bought from the others, so the total is the economy rather than the sum of all invoices. It is the same “don’t double-count the flour” rule from Primer 03, applied at national scale.

1 The four words in the subtitle that change what you are looking at

The tool’s subtitle reads: “Share of gross domestic product at basic prices, by industry — chained (2017) dollars, seasonally adjusted at annual rates.” Every one of those is doing work, and three of them are places people misread the chart.

TermWhat it meansWhat it changes
At basic pricesValue added measured before taxes on products (GST, excise, duties) and after subsidies on products.This is not the same $ figure as headline GDP at market prices, which is bigger. So do not compare $2.36T here against a “Canada is a $3 trillion economy” headline — different measure, not a revision.
Chained (2017) dollarsReal, not nominal — price changes stripped out, with each period valued at the previous period’s prices and the growth rates linked together.You are seeing quantities, not price effects. It also means the components do not add exactly to the total — which is why the tool’s own footnote says shares are computed against the published all-industries total and sum to slightly less than 100%. That is the method, not an error.
Seasonally adjustedThe predictable calendar pattern — construction in February, retail in December — has been removed.Lets you compare May to April without pretending winter is a recession.
At annual ratesOne month’s output multiplied up to a yearly pace.$2.36T is not what Canada produced in May. It is what Canada would produce in a year at May’s pace.
“Other”The tool bundles other services (except public administration), arts/entertainment/recreation, and management of companies into one 2.75% slice.Fine for a donut, but remember it when comparing against the U.S. table, which splits those three out.

Source stated in the tool: Statistics Canada, Table 36-10-0434-01, Gross domestic product (GDP) at basic prices, by industry, monthly; figures as published in The Daily, July 31, 2026, reference month May 2026.

The chained-dollar footnote is worth understanding, not skipping. Under chain weighting each period is valued at the immediately preceding period’s prices, so the weights stay current as the economy changes — but the arithmetic consequence is that real components stop being additive. If you add the seventeen industry figures you will not land exactly on 2,362,482. Every national statistical agency has this problem and every one of them handles it the same way. It is the price of keeping the weights honest.

2 The Canadian numbers, in full

Seventeen industries, ranked. The useful way to read this is in three blocks: what is bigger than you expect at the top, the middle band where seven industries sit within two points of each other, and the tail.

Top slice
13.2%

Real estate, rental & leasing. Mostly imputed rent — the rent homeowners notionally pay themselves, which Primer 03 flags as the single most important imputation in the accounts. It is not a measure of house sales.

The surprise
7.2%

Construction is as big as the entire federal, provincial and municipal public administration. Housing is not a sector of the Canadian economy; it is one of its load-bearing walls.

Services vs goods
~70%

Add real estate, trade, health, finance, public admin, professional, education, transport, information, admin, accommodation and “other”: services dominate. Goods-producing industries — manufacturing, construction, mining, utilities, agriculture — are roughly a quarter.

The one to notice
3.5%

Information & cultural industries. The entire tech, telecom, media and software sector is a third the size of real estate — and this is the number that matters most for the productivity argument.

The caveat you must state if you use this in class. The two series are not perfectly comparable, for two reasons. First, Canada’s shares are of GDP at basic prices, the U.S. shares are of GDP at market prices — so the denominators differ slightly and every Canadian share is nudged marginally upward relative to its U.S. counterpart. Second, and more seriously, the public sector is carved up differently: Canada reports Public administration (7.21%) and Educational services (5.25%) separately, while the BEA reports a single Government sector (11.0%) that contains public schools and much public health delivery, leaving only private education in its 1.1% “educational services” line. Those rows are genuinely not comparable and I have left them out of the chart rather than pretend otherwise. The fourteen rows that remain are the same NAICS sectors on both sides.

4 What the mix means for the Session 2 debate

Industry structure is not trivia. It determines how shocks travel, which is the whole question in today’s session. Five things fall straight out of the comparison.

FindingWhy it matters today
1 · Mining, oil & gas is 3.8× the U.S. share
5.29% vs 1.4%		This is why the Hormuz energy shock is not the same event in Ottawa as in Washington. For the U.S. it is a pure cost shock. For Canada it is a cost shock and a terms-of-trade gain — 5.3% of the economy is selling the thing that got expensive. It is also why the Canadian GDP deflator is far more oil-sensitive than Canadian CPI. Use this to make the tailwind argument precise rather than hand-wavy.
2 · Construction is 1.7× the U.S. share
7.21% vs 4.3% — and as large as all public administration		Monetary policy reaches the real economy through housing faster and harder in Canada than almost anywhere. A bigger construction sector plus mortgage interest sitting inside the CPI basket means rate moves transmit through a wider pipe. It is the mechanical reason the mortgage renewal wall is a national macro risk here and a sectoral story in the U.S.
3 · The U.S. has 1.6× the Information share
5.6% vs 3.53%		The most important row in the table. Statistics Canada found AI adoption is highest in information and cultural industries and in professional, scientific and technical services. The U.S. simply has more of its economy sitting in those sectors. So even with identical firm-level adoption behaviour, the U.S. gets a larger aggregate productivity gain purely from its mix. The projected AI productivity boost — up to 1.5pp a year for the U.S. against 0.4–1.1pp for Canada — is not only a story about Canadian firms being slower. It is partly arithmetic.
4 · Finance, real estate and manufacturing are near-identical
within one point on all three		Kills the lazy version of the pessimist case. Canada is not a resource colony with a real economy bolted on; it has a U.S.-sized financial sector, a U.S.-sized property sector, and proportionally almost as much manufacturing. If you are arguing the optimist side, this is your structural evidence. The Canadian problem is not what it makes — it is who it sells to and how much it produces per hour.
5 · The mix is tilted away from where productivity growth comes from
AI-exposed sectors: Canada ~20.9% vs U.S. ~24.8%		Adding information + professional/scientific/technical + finance & insurance + administrative support, Canada has 20.9% of its economy in the sectors with the highest AI adoption; the U.S. has 24.8% — about a fifth more. Meanwhile Canada carries more construction, more resource extraction and a larger public administration, which are exactly the Baumol-bound sectors where measured productivity barely moves and where statisticians measure output by cost. The industry mix is itself part of the productivity gap.

The 20.9% / 24.8% figures are my own sums from the two tables (Canada: 3.53 + 7.10 + 7.71 + 2.59; U.S.: 5.6 + 8.1 + 8.0 + 3.1). “AI-exposed” is my grouping, chosen to match the sectors StatCan identifies as highest-adopting — it is a reasonable definition, not an official one.

The line to use in class
“People describe Canada as a resource economy and the U.S. as a tech economy, and the data only half agrees. Finance, real estate and manufacturing are within a point of each other. The real gaps are three: Canada has 3.8 times the mining share, 1.7 times the construction share, and the U.S. has 1.6 times the information share. That last one is why the AI productivity projections differ — it is not only that Canadian firms adopt more slowly, it is that Canada has less of the economy sitting in the sectors where adoption pays.”

Traps when you quote this chart

“Canada’s GDP is $2.36 trillion”That is GDP at basic prices, annualised from one month. Headline GDP at market prices is larger. Say which you mean.
Adding the industry figures to check the totalChained dollars are not additive. The tool says so in its own footnote. Shares sum to slightly under 100%.
“Real estate is 13% of GDP, the housing bubble is enormous”Most of that line is imputed rent — the rent homeowners notionally pay themselves. It is not transactions, and Primer 03 flags it as the accounts’ most important imputation.
Comparing Canadian public administration to U.S. governmentDifferent carve-ups. The BEA’s Government sector absorbs public education and much public health; Canada reports those separately.
“Canada doesn’t manufacture anything”8.45% vs 9.4% in the U.S. The problem is concentration of customers, not absence of industry.
Treating one month as a trendMay 2026, seasonally adjusted, and subject to revision. Primer 04: never build a strong conclusion on one release.

Q&A log

Follow-up questions get appended here, newest first.

Q1 · If real estate is the biggest industry in both countries, why does anyone call the U.S. a tech economy?

Because share of value added and share of growth, market capitalisation and productivity gains are four different things, and the tech story lives in the last three. Information is only 5.6% of U.S. value added — but it is a large share of the increase in output, an enormous share of the stock market, and the source of most measured productivity growth. A donut chart of levels is almost designed to hide that.

Example — why the levels chart understates it
The BEA’s Q1 2026 release named information as one of the leading contributors to the rise in real GDP, alongside federal government and professional/scientific/technical services. A sector can be 5.6% of the level and a much larger share of the change — that is exactly the difference between this tool and the contributions-to-growth tool on the Canvas page.
Level share ≠ growth contribution ≠ market cap ≠ productivity impact
Which is the honest answer to “is 2 percentage points of information share really such a big deal?” On levels, it looks small. On growth and on where AI adoption concentrates, it is the whole argument.
