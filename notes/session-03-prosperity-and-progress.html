The whole session, in one picture

In one line
Economies have grown for 200 years and will probably keep growing. GDP is how we count that, and it is good at counting production and bad at counting lives.
The class question: the professor will show a long GDP dataset and ask why some places are rich and some are poor. Sections 1 to 3 are for that. Sections 4 to 8 are for the second half, on whether GDP is worth chasing at all.

The long run

sections 1–3

1 The hockey stick

In one line
For almost all of human history, living standards did not move. Then, around 1800, they went vertical, and they have not stopped.

Your grandparents were richer than theirs because of the last two centimetres of that line. Growth is recent, not natural. Nothing in the first 1,800 years of the chart says it has to continue.

The same period produced the gap between countries

Before 1800 the richest country was maybe four times the poorest. Growth did not arrive everywhere at once, and the countries that got it first are still ahead.

The line that opens the class
Ask when the gap opened, not why it exists. A 55 to 1 spread from a 4 to 1 start is not poor countries falling behind. It is rich countries pulling away. That reframes the question from "what is wrong with the DR Congo" to "what happened in Britain in 1800 and why did it not travel".

2 Why the rate is everything

In one line
Growth rates that sound identical are not. Two percent doubles living standards in a working life. One percent takes two generations.
1

Get the doubling time in your head

Divide 70 by the growth rate and you get the years to double. It is an approximation, and it is accurate enough to use out loud in class.

Doubling time  =  70  ÷  growth rate (% per year)

  at 3.0%   →  70 ÷ 3.0  =  23 years   China, catch-up decades
  at 2.0%   →  70 ÷ 2.0  =  35 years   one working life
  at 1.0%   →  70 ÷ 1.0  =  70 years   two generations
  at 0.5%   →  70 ÷ 0.5  =  140 years  never, in a life

The difference between 2% and 1% does not feel like much in a quarterly release. Over a career it is the whole argument.

2

Put a dollar figure on one percentage point

Start a Canadian at roughly today's output per person and run the two rates out fifty years.

Level after n years  =  start × (1 + g)n

  at 2%:   $55,000 × 1.0250  =  $55,000 × 2.692  =  $148,000
  at 1%:   $55,000 × 1.0150  =  $55,000 × 1.645  =  $ 90,500
                                              ──────────
  One percentage point, over fifty years:        $57,500  per person, per year

That gap is larger than the entire starting level. Nothing a government does in a single term matters as much as moving the trend rate by half a point.

3

Then subtract population

Total GDP growth tells you the economy is busier. Only per person growth tells you anyone is better off. Canada has been failing this test.

Growth per person  ≈  GDP growth  −  population growth

  Canada, roughly 2023:   +1.5%  −  +3.2%  =  −1.7%   illustrative rounding

  Statistics Canada's actual reading: real GDP per person fell in
  five of the six quarters to mid 2024, back near its 2017 level.

This is why "the economy grew" and "we got poorer" were both true in Canada at the same time.

3 Why some places are rich and some are poor

In one line
Output comes from tools, skills and know-how. But what decides how much of each a country gets is its rules, and rules are the deep answer.

Know-how does most of the work. Tools and skills run into diminishing returns, a tenth machine adds less than the second. Only better ways of doing things keep paying forever, which is why the rich-country line in section 1 has not flattened out.

The four explanations, and what each gets right

Luck and timingWho industrialised first compounds foreverThe 4 to 1 to 55 to 1 spreadSome latecomers caught up completely

Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson won the 2024 Nobel for the rules argument. If you name one framework in class, name that one.

Grounded
Korea in 1953 was poorer than Ghana. Today South Korea is around $50,000 per person and North Korea is somewhere near $1,500. Same peninsula, same language, same starting point, one variable changed. No geography story survives that comparison.

4 What GDP actually is

In one line
The market value of everything finished that a country makes inside its borders in a year. Three different ways of adding it up, one total.

Why only the final sale counts

Counting every transaction would count the flour twice, once as flour and again inside the bread. GDP counts what each step added, which comes to the same thing as the final price.

Worked, a loaf of bread
                                      sale     value added
  Farmer sells wheat to the miller    $0.40       $0.40
  Miller sells flour to the baker     $0.90       $0.50
  Baker sells bread to you            $2.50       $1.60
                                                ───────
  Sum of value added                            $2.50
  Final sale price                              $2.50   ✓ same number, both routes

  Adding up every sale instead:        $3.80             counts the wheat three times
Two routes, one answer. That is the check to do in your head any time someone quotes a gross turnover figure as economic impact.
The four boundaries, each drawn on purpose
BoundaryInOutWhy
Where, not whoToyota's Ontario plantA Canadian mine in ChileGDP is location, GNI is ownership
Money changed handsPaying a daycareMinding your own childUnpaid work has no price to record
Finished goods onlyThe breadThe flour in itAvoids double counting
Gross, not netReplacing a worn machineNothing subtracted for wearProduction accounting, not a balance sheet
Invented, not discovered. Kuznets built this in the 1930s so governments could see how deep the Depression was, then it was retooled in the war to plan military output. It was designed to measure production capacity. Every complaint that it ignores wellbeing is really a complaint that it is doing the job it was built for.

5 From nominal to real

In one line
A dollar figure rises when you make more things or when things cost more. Real GDP separates the two, and only the first one counts as growth.
1

Divide out the price level

The deflator is an average of every price in the economy, each weighted by how much of it gets made. Divide nominal GDP by it and what is left is quantities.

Real GDP  =  nominal GDP  ÷  (deflator ÷ 100)

  Year 1:   $3,000B  ÷  1.000   =  $3,000B   deflator set to 100
  Year 2:   $3,180B  ÷  1.030   =  $3,087B   prices up 3.0%

  Nominal growth  =  3,180 ÷ 3,000 − 1  =  +6.0%
  Real growth     =  3,087 ÷ 3,000 − 1  =  +2.9%
  Quick version   =  6.0% − 3.0%           =   +3.0%   close, always a shade high

Half of that headline six percent was just prices. When economists say growth, they always mean the 2.9%.

2

Know why the weights get updated

Old accounts priced everything at one base year forever. That works while the economy stands still and breaks when it does not.

Fixed weights:  value every year at 1930 prices
                → horses still carry 1930's weight in 2026

Chain weights:  value each year at last year's prices,
                then link the growth rates together
                → weights stay current as products come and go

Statistics Canada chains. It is the reason real GDP levels from different vintages never quite line up.

Carry this forward. Any bias in measuring prices flows straight into growth with the sign flipped. Overstate inflation by half a point and you have understated real growth by half a point. Section 7 is a list of the ways that happens.

6 Where GDP stops

In one line
The boundary follows the money. If no one paid for it, it is invisible, however valuable it was.

Because only crossings of that line get recorded, work can move without any work being done. Women entering paid employment in the 1970s moved meals and childcare from inside the household to the market and raised measured GDP. Self checkout and online banking move it back and quietly lower it.

The cash economy sits awkwardly. Unreported renovations, tips and drug sales are market activity and belong in GDP in principle. Statistics Canada estimates rather than observes them: about 2.5% of GDP, some $72 billion in 2023, with residential construction the biggest single piece.

Plus four quieter measurement biases, all in the same direction

Together these probably understate real growth by a few tenths of a point a year. Enough to matter over fifty years, not enough to change any trend.

The 1850 version, still the best one
Bastiat's broken window. A vandal smashes a shop window, the glazier gets paid, output rises. The town is not better off, it has a new window where it had an intact one, and the shopkeeper's money would otherwise have bought shoes. The unseen alternative is the cost. GDP records the repair honestly and says nothing about the shoes.

8 Average versus typical

In one line
GDP per person is total output divided by heads. In an unequal country that describes nobody who actually lives there.

Both sat near $7,000 to $8,000 per person in the mid 2010s. Thailand's median income was more than twice South Africa's. In South Africa the top tenth takes about 65% of income and the bottom half shares 6%. Poverty, housing and life expectancy all follow the median, not the average.

Who gains when real GDP rises with inflation running
GainsWhyLosesWhy
Business ownersProfits track a strong economyWage earnersPay adjusts slowly to prices
New hiresIncome where there was noneRetireesFixed payments, rising prices
BorrowersReal debt shrinksSaversStored wealth buys less

"Real GDP rose" means output beat prices on average. Whether it beat them for any given household is a separate question the number cannot answer.

The questions

9 Will the next generation be richer?

Guide to preparation, question 1
Do you believe the next generation will be “richer” than yours? Why or why not?
Say this

Yes on the level, and I am not confident about the rate. Materially they will consume more than we do, because every rich country has managed that in every fifty year window since 1800 outside war and state collapse. But the Canadian arithmetic has turned. Output per person fell in five of six quarters to mid 2024 and sits near its 2017 level. At two percent they double in thirty five years. At half a percent they never do. So the honest answer is that the sign is close to certain and the size is genuinely at risk, and most of the improvement they do get will land in things GDP counts badly: health outcomes, free digital services, product quality. They will feel less rich than the statistics say, and be more rich than the statistics say, at the same time.

The argument, in four moves

1
Start from the base rate, not from the moodSection 1 is 200 years of one direction. A forecast of decline has to explain what breaks a two century streak. Pessimism has to carry the burden of proof here, not optimism.
2
Separate the level from the rateNostalgia confuses the two. Yglesias's whole point: the 1960s felt better because growth was faster, not because life was better. Houses were 25% smaller, half as many cars per person, dishwashers were a luxury.
3
Name what could actually break itAn ageing workforce shrinking hours worked, weak business investment per worker, housing costs absorbing the gains, and climate damage that repairs itself into GDP while making people worse off.
4
Close on the measurement pointThe measured gain will understate the real one, because new goods and quality improvements are exactly what GDP handles worst, and they are a growing share of what improves.
Richer on what, exactly
DimensionDirection for the next generationConfidence

If someone argues the other side
The strongest pessimist case is not that growth stops, it is that the composition changes. More of output goes to replacing what climate and ageing destroy rather than to new capacity. Consumption per person keeps rising while the wealth behind it thins out. That is a balance sheet argument, and GDP is a flow measure, so GDP cannot see it. Adjusted Net Savings can.

10 What “rich” means, and whether GDP catches it

Guide to preparation, question 2
What do you mean by “rich” anyway? How does your definition of rich align with economists’ measure of GDP or GDP per capita?
Say this, roughly 150 words

Rich means the number of lives you could choose. Not what you own, but what you can refuse: a job, a city, a bad marriage, a medical bill. On that definition money is one input among several, alongside health, time and the security to take a risk. GDP per capita captures maybe half of it, and captures that half well. It tracks consumption closely over long horizons, and consumption buys most of the options. Where it breaks is predictable. It is an average, so it says nothing about the person in the middle. It counts production, so a flood makes it rise. It counts what was sold, so it misses the hours you do not have to work and the services you get for free. So I use it as a floor, not a score: below a certain GDP per capita nothing else is achievable, above it the number stops being informative.

The move that lands in class. Do not argue GDP is wrong. Argue it is a floor, not a score. Nothing on the list above is reachable at $1,500 a head, so GDP per capita is close to a perfect measure of wellbeing at the bottom of the distribution and a weak one at the top. That is why the same statistic can be indispensable for the DR Congo and nearly useless for Denmark.
The counter to have ready
Someone will say GDP growth is a trojan horse that drives consumption and climate damage. The reply is that GDP is a thermometer, not a policy. Nothing in the national accounts tells a government to maximise the number. And the countries that cut emissions per unit of output are the rich ones, because the cleanup is bought with the growth. Degrowth arguments usually want a different distribution and mistake it for wanting a different measure.

Q&A log

September 16, 2026
If GDP misses this much, why does the course keep using it?

The alternatives in section 11 are each better at one thing and worse at being a common language. GDP's value is not accuracy, it is comparability. You can put Canada in 1985 next to Poland in 2024 and the number means the same thing, which is not true of any wellbeing index built so far. The right posture is the one Primer 4 lands on: read GDP alongside employment, inflation, distribution and environmental damage, and never on its own.
