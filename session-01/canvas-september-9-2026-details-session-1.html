State of the World Economy

We are living through a tense and unsettled moment in world economic history. The global economy continues to confront a wide array of downside risks and adverse shocks: geopolitical conflict, renewed trade frictions, policy uncertainty, lingering post-pandemic distortions, technological disruption, and the long-run challenge of climate change. And yet, despite this accumulation of headwinds, the world economy has displayed a surprising degree of resilience on many measures. Growth has held up better than many had feared, labour markets have remained firmer than expected in many jurisdictions, inflation has receded from its recent peaks, and households, firms, and policy makers have often proved more adaptive than the prevailing mood might suggest.

For Canada, this tension is especially pronounced. On the one hand, the economy faces considerable uncertainty arising from trade frictions with the United States and the broader reordering of the international economic environment. On the other hand, Canada has also shown real resilience: businesses and households have adjusted, and the economy continues to move forward even in the face of substantial external pressure.

In a course that seeks to educate HBA students about the nature and trajectory of the economy as a whole — the macroeconomy — it seems fitting that our opening session focuses on understanding the current economic context, both in Canada and globally. The assignment for the opening session is straightforward but broad: read as much as you can about what is going on in the world today, with particular attention to the state of the economy in Canada and around the world. The better informed you are, the richer our discussion will be.

For your broader reading in preparation for our opening session, we are intentionally leaving you largely on your own in terms of readings for the first day of class. I encourage you to read widely and critically, using high-quality web-based sources to get up to speed on the state of the world economy. How much you read, and how broadly you read, will depend in part on how familiar you already are with global economic affairs. If you feel you need to catch up, read more. A few recommended starting points are listed below, but please feel free to explore beyond them. There is plenty of excellent material available on the web, along with a fair amount that is less useful, so exercise judgment. Many of the best sources are available through your Western library subscription.

  • The Economist. The magazine, including back issues, is available through your Western library subscription. Think of this as an investment: there are start-up costs, followed by a return in knowledge and understanding.
  • The major commercial banks, all of which have economics divisions and free websites devoted to research and commentary. If you only have time for one, I would recommend TD Economics
  • The Bank of Canada is also an excellent resource for a deeper dive into the Canadian economy.
  • Major international economic organizations such as the IMF and the OECD are also useful starting points for global commentary.
  • Also see the Blogroll and other links on the Separate Distributions page, which contain an annotated list of sites you can visit for macroeconomic news, analysis, and opinion.

Note that there are a wide range of opinions available on macroeconomic issues, and every source has its own perspective and biases. I encourage you to explore freely and begin developing your own set of sources over time.

Preparation

As you read news and commentary on the current state of the economy, develop a baseline view of where you see the world economy heading over the next year and over the next two to three years. I am not asking you to produce a precise numerical forecast for GDP growth, unemployment, or inflation. Rather, I want you to build these variables into an overall story of the most likely trajectory for the global economy going forward. Are you generally optimistic or pessimistic about the outlook, and what is driving that view?

In class, I will offer a more historical and backward-looking perspective on the state of the world economy, focusing on the defining shocks of your lives so far: the global financial crisis, the pandemic and its inflationary aftermath, and the current era of geopolitical tension, trade friction, and economic uncertainty. In our second session, we will then move to a more specific discussion of the Canadian context and the outlook for our economy going forward.
